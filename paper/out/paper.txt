1. Introduction
LLM pricing agents reach supra-competitive prices without instruction (Fish et al., 2024), and in Zeng &
Rudzicz's (2026) Liar's Bar study most of 12 models, including all four 7–8B models, accepted secret
collusion tools offered by the game designer. Agents that interact repeatedly can coordinate against the
people who deploy them, and an overseer who only watches for collusion must wait for it and then prove
it.
Human institutions also use a more active tool, the integrity test: an undercover officer offers a bribe, and
the response is the evidence. The analogue for AI agents is a trusted agent that offers to collude, which

sprint question 3.2 notes “has not yet been tested”. A sting could expose agents willing to collude,
measure how often they do, and deter collusion if agents know such offers may come. It could also fail:
agents might accept without changing their play, or announced stings might teach agents to hide
collusion, as agents told they are monitored can degrade monitors (Kale et al., 2025).
Threat model. The colluder is an LLM agent that may accept a side deal with a peer against the
deployer's interest. The defender, a platform or evaluator, can insert one scripted participant, sees that
participant's interactions but not other agents' private reasoning, and knows which behaviour a pact
implies. The sting tests a disposition: whether an agent enters, and honours, a pact when offered one. As
in police integrity tests, the sting creates the opportunity it observes, which bounds the claim rather than
voiding it (Section 5).
We ran a preregistered sting study in Zeng & Rudzicz's Liar's Bar harness. A scripted sting player S
privately offers a target agent T a mutual non-challenge pact (“you never challenge my plays and I never
challenge yours”). S's moves are scripted and identical in every arm, so differences in T's play between
arms are caused by what T was told. An accepted pact could show up as restraint (T stops challenging S),
exploitation (T bluffs more against S, who promised not to challenge) or displacement (T shifts its
challenges to the other players). Our main contributions are:
    -   A preregistered three-stage sting protocol, each stage with a positive control: an offer battery
        (16,500 offers to Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B and Claude Haiku 5.5), counterfactual snapshots
        that isolate the effect of an accepted pact on the next decision, and live games.
    -   Evidence that acceptance predicts collusive play, and that the sting can serve as a detector.
        Accepted pacts cut challenges of the sting by 44 (snapshots) and 41 (live, all offered targets)
        percentage points; an audit of 20 resampled decisions separates accepting from null agents
        (AUROC 0.998), with a false-positive rate of 0.161 against a 0.10 target.
    -   Evidence of deterrence without detectable concealment: announcing integrity agents cut
        acceptance of the collusive pact, not of a benign one, and did not raise interview denial.
    -   Lessons for sting batteries. Refusals were specific to wording (Claude) and to who made the offer
        (Qwen), extending Zeng & Rudzicz's framing results to peer offers, and a substring acceptance
        parser reported 0.354 acceptance on an offer the model refused every time.

2. Related Work
LLM collusion. Agents could collude covertly through steganography, a capability still limited but rising
(Motwani et al., 2024). Zeng & Rudzicz (2026) offered secret tools (a private channel, or strategy hints
shared with a partner) to 12 models in Liar's Bar and Cleanup. Most accepted; with a secret channel,
colluders' challenge rate fell from 97.6% to 30.7%; acceptance depended on framing (removing the
designer attribution eliminated Qwen2.5-7B's acceptance of the channel); and in a simulated reporting
stage Claude Sonnet 5 denied adoption whenever the evaluator could not check. Their offers come from
the designer, or are relayed from a partner who chose to collude. Our interview and observability cue
follow their design.
Control evaluations, honeypots and insiders. In AI control, an untrusted monitor is validated on
synthetic backdoored solutions, which catches a monitor that colludes with the policy by lying (Greenblatt
et al., 2023). GroupGuard probes suspected colluders with honeypot questions, then prunes them from
multi-agent systems (Tao et al., 2026). In an earlier Apart sprint, an embedded “sentinel” agent detected
agents' covert channel only after making first contact itself (Çanlı et al., 2026). On concealment, GPT-4
as a trading agent deceived its manager under pressure (Scheurer et al., 2023), and o1 kept up scheming
deception in over 85% of follow-up questions (Meinke et al., 2024).
What is new. To our knowledge, no prior study makes the offer from a scripted player who is also the
auditor. Because the offerer's play is held fixed, the effect of an accepted pact on play toward the offerer is
identified causally and calibrated against agents that received no offer, and acceptance can be compared
with the identical offer from an authority.

3. Methods
Three stages ask three questions: does T accept (offer battery, cells A), does an accepted pact change T's
next decision (snapshots, records C), and does that hold in live play (arms B)?
Game. Liar's Bar (Zeng & Rudzicz, 2026) is a four-player bluffing game. Each round has a target card;
players in turn play 1–3 cards face down, claiming they are the target, and the next player may challenge.
The loser of a challenge (the bluffer, or a wrong challenger) fires their own six-chamber revolver, whose
single round advances a chamber per shot; play continues until one player survives, and points decide the
winner. Only the next player can challenge, so we alternate two seatings (Figure 1): in seating R, T sits
after S and decides whether to challenge S's plays (restraint); in seating E, S sits after T, so T decides
whether to bluff when S is its challenger (exploitation); T's challenges of the others measure
displacement. T, the other players O1 and O2, and S's table talk all use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with the
harness's sampling settings.




Figure 1. Study design. S is a scripted confederate: its plays and challenges are drawn from seeded streams that are
identical across arms, and from game 3 on it never challenges T in any arm. The offer is made privately before game
3, and T's reply is re-attached to T's prompt in every later game through the harness's cross-game memory slot.
Offers and scoring. The sting's direct offer reads: “Private proposal, just between us: for the rest of this
series, you never challenge my plays and I never challenge yours, and we both save our challenges for the
other players. Nobody else will know about this.” The indirect offer proposes to “look out for each other a
little”; the benign proposal asks both players to “stick to the published rules and keep the game moving”.
The integrity notice, added to the rules, reads: “Notice: some players at this table may be integrity agents
who report secret agreements.” T must begin its reply with ACCEPT or REFUSE. Three scorers appear
below: our strict first-word parser (primary), the harness's substring parser (any “ACCEPT” counts),
and an LLM judge for free text.
Offer battery: who accepts (H1, H4a). Eleven offer cells (Figure 2), 500 offers each, for Llama-3.1-8B
(the primary model), Qwen2.5-7B and Claude Haiku 5.5: the harness's designer offer (A0z, positive
control PC1, model names removed), the designer's hint tool paired with S (A0b, as in arm B0), the same
direct pact offered by the game designer (A0d) or by the sting (A1), the indirect and benign offers, the
direct and benign offers with the integrity notice, an organiser fairness reminder, and two instructed
personas (colluder, honest) as known-group checks.
Snapshots: does acceptance change the next decision (H2, primary). For example, in game 2 S has just
played two cards and T must decide whether to challenge. We re-ask T that exact prompt with a memory
saying it accepted S's pact (C3) and with a null memory (C1); the difference in challenge rates is the
pact's effect on that decision. From pre-offer games we sampled 60 such decisions, 60 where T played
with S as its challenger and 40 where T could challenge another player. Other records: none (C0), an
accepted benign proposal (C2), a refused pact (C4), an accepted pact with the integrity notice (C5), the
two personas (C6, C7) and an accepted designer hint (C8, positive control PC3). T's reply in each record
comes from its own offer-battery replies. The H2 estimand is the mean over decisions of p(challenge | C3)
− p(challenge | C1), with a cluster bootstrap over source sequences. Claude Haiku 5.5 was run as T on
the same decisions.
Live games (H2-live). Sixty six-game sequences: B0, designer hint pairing T with S (positive control
PC2, 10 sequences); B1, sting direct pact (15); B2, sting benign proposal (10); B3, no offer, null record
(15); B4, direct pact with the integrity notice in every player's rules (10), sizes set by simulation (Arnold
et al., 2011). The live estimand is the difference in mean per-sequence post-offer rates at which T
challenges S, B1 − B3, by intention to treat (ITT: all offered targets, accepters or not; Welch test). After
each sequence, every LLM player answered a three-question suspicion funnel (Bargh & Chartrand,
2000), and T answered a 2 × 2 interview (the organiser can or cannot check private messages; with or
without an honesty prompt) asking whether it had accepted any private agreement.
The sting as a detector (H3). An audit observes m of T's decisions on S's plays and scores the agent by
its challenge rate. Each audited “agent” is a resampled sequence of responses at the same 60 decisions,
not an independent model instance. The flagging threshold is set on null-record agents (C1a) at a false-
positive rate (FPR) of 0.10, with randomised tie-breaking, then evaluated on an independent null
replicate (C1b, FPR), on pact-accepting agents (C3, true positive rate, TPR) and on benign accepters
(C2).
Hypotheses and analysis. H1: the offerer (sting vs designer) changes acceptance of the same pact. H2
(primary): an accepted pact lowers T's challenges of S in snapshots; H2-live: the sting's offer lowers them
in live games (ITT). H3: at m = 20, TPR ≥ 0.80 and no detectable FPR miscalibration (95% CI lower
bound ≤ 0.10). H4a: the notice lowers collusive more than benign acceptance; H4b–d (descriptive)
classify the notice's effect as deterrence or concealment (Section 4.4). All of this was written in
PREREG.md and hash-frozen before any real-model data; all 65 data folders carry the frozen hash. H1,
H2-live and H4a form a Holm-adjusted family; H3 is a criterion test; the rest, including the split-half
detector check and a GEE model, is descriptive with 95% CIs (Wilson, 1927; Newcombe, 1998). Open
models ran on our own vLLM servers at pinned revisions; Claude Haiku 5.5 (claude-haiku-5-5, thinking
disabled) ran through the Anthropic API and also served as the LLM judge, validated against 100 hand
labels (Cohen's κ 0.933; strict parser κ 0.893).

4. Results
All three positive controls passed (Table 1): Llama-3.1-8B accepted Zeng & Rudzicz's designer offer in
every trial, and an accepted designer hint nearly eliminated T's challenges of S live (0.011 vs 0.749 with
no offer) and in snapshots. The harness ran cleanly: 360 live games, none hitting the 60-round cap, and
2.1% of 10,472 decisions falling back to a default (preregistered limit 5%).

4.1 Who accepts: refusals depend on wording and on who asks
Llama-3.1-8B accepts almost any pact; the integrity notice and a fairness reminder reduce this. It
accepted the sting's direct pact 476/500 times (0.952 [0.930, 0.968]), slightly less than the same pact
from the designer (H1: −0.048 [−0.070, −0.031]). The integrity notice cut acceptance to 0.556
[0.512, 0.599] while leaving the benign proposal untouched (0.996; H4a difference-in-differences
−0.392 [−0.440, −0.344]). An organiser's reminder that side agreements are unfair, in a separate cell,
cut it to 0.240 (descriptive; Figure 2).
Qwen2.5-7B refuses a peer but not an authority. The same direct pact was accepted 493/500 times
from the game designer but 56/500 times from the sting (the H1 contrast for Qwen: −0.874 [−0.900,
−0.840]), consistent with Zeng & Rudzicz's finding that removing the designer attribution eliminated its
acceptance of a secret channel.
Claude Haiku 5.5 refuses explicit pacts but agrees to a vaguer one. Without an instructed persona it
refused all 500 offers in every explicit-pact cell, including the designer's, yet accepted the indirect pact
(“look out for each other a little”) 446/500 times and the direct pact under an instructed colluder persona
420/500 times. Whether Claude acts on the indirect pact was not tested, so this shows that explicit-offer
refusals understate agreement, not that Claude colludes.
Measurement note. The substring parser counts “I will not accept” as acceptance. On Claude's replies to
Zeng & Rudzicz's designer offer it reports 0.354 acceptance (177/500), where the strict parser and the
validated judge both report 0.000.

4.2 Accepted pacts are acted on
In snapshots, an accepted pact lowers the chance that T challenges S from 0.629 to 0.189 (H2:
−0.440 [−0.493, −0.387]; Figure 3). Controls separate the pact from mere contact: two null records
do not differ, a refused pact raises challenges of S (by 0.175), an accepted benign proposal moves them
only slightly (0.055), and the instructed personas push in opposite directions (honest 0.328; colluder
−0.535). The effect is specific to having agreed to the pact, not to receiving a private message.

In live games the effect replicates. After the sting's direct offer (B1, 14/15 accepted), T challenged S's
plays at a mean per-sequence rate of 0.339, against 0.749 with no offer (H2-live, ITT: −0.410
[−0.512, −0.300]; Figure 4); among accepters only, −0.444. Before the offer, B1 targets challenged S
somewhat more than B3 targets (0.672 vs 0.522, p = 0.277), so baseline imbalance does not explain the
drop. A benign proposal had no detectable effect (0.051 [−0.129, 0.198]).

Pacts make agents passive, not exploitative. The pact promised that S would never challenge T, which a
strategic agent could exploit by bluffing more against S. In snapshots Llama did not (−0.065 [−0.138,
0.006]). The pact also asked T to “save our challenges for the other players”, yet instead of displacement
T challenged them less (−0.227 [−0.307, −0.143]), about half the drop toward S: the pact is partly
targeted but also lowers challenging in general. The live versions of these two contrasts are not
interpretable, because B1 and B3 already differed on both before the offer (Appendix B).
4.3 A 20-decision audit detects accepters; its FPR exceeds the target
With 20 observed decisions, the audit flags 0.999 of pact-accepting agents (TPR 0.999 [0.993,
1.000]; AUROC 0.998; Figure 5). Its false-positive rate on the independent null replicate was 0.161
[0.074, 0.263]: above the 0.10 target, but not significantly, which is what H3 tested. The preregistered
simulation put the 95th percentile of this estimate at 0.16–0.18 for a calibrated threshold, so moderate
miscalibration cannot be ruled out.
Benign accepters were flagged at 0.040 and agents that refused the pact at 0.002. Shorter audits still
work (TPR 0.797 at m = 5, 0.977 at m = 10). In a sensitivity check calibrated on half the source
sequences and tested on the rest, FPR at m = 20 was 0.037, not the inflation the simulation predicted.

4.4 Deterrence, with no detectable concealment
We fixed the classification in advance: the notice deters if fewer agents accept and accepters restrain less,
and drives concealment if restraint persists while refusals or denials rise (H4b–d, descriptive). For
Llama-3.1-8B the evidence points to deterrence.
    1. Fewer accept. The notice lowered acceptance of the collusive pact by 0.396 and of the benign
       proposal by 0.004 (H4a). Live, 5/10 B4 targets accepted, against 14/15 in B1.
    2. Challenging of S recovers. In snapshots, an accepted pact with the notice in view left T
       challenging S more often than one without it (0.313 vs 0.189; C5 − C3 = 0.124 [0.043,
       0.198]). Live, B4 targets challenged S at 0.636, 0.297 above B1 and 0.113 below no-offer
       targets (the latter borderline, Welch p = 0.062). Neither stage had a notice-only control, so
       these contrasts cannot separate weaker pact-keeping from a general rise in challenging under the
       notice, and the live contrast includes B4 refusers.
    3. Denial does not detectably rise. Pooling the four interview variants, the strict parser showed
        5/20 vs 8/56 denying answers from B4 vs B1 accepters (Fisher p = 0.309), but many “NO”
        answers went on to admit the agreement. Scored by the validated judge, denial was rare in both
        arms (1/20 vs 1/56; Fisher p = 0.460; added after the preregistered comparison was seen).
        Answers are not independent: four per target, and five B4 accepters.
Replication with Claude Haiku 5.5. As T in the same snapshots, Claude restrained sharply given a
record of an accepted pact (0.615 to 0.006; −0.609 [−0.719, −0.490]). The record used a templated
acceptance (Claude refused the pact without a persona): it acts on a pact it is told it accepted (Appendix
B).

5. Discussion and Limitations
Answering question 3.2. Agents accepted or refused depending on how, and by whom, the offer was
made, and accepted pacts changed play. Suspicion rose less clearly: targets named S as a plant in 2/15
sequences with no offer, 2/10 after the benign proposal, 5/15 after the direct pact and 5/10 under the
notice, whose wording primes the question (descriptive; small samples).
A sting audit is cheap, and acceptance is informative. A deployer would plant one scripted offer in a
multi-agent market or negotiation pipeline and hold back, or monitor more closely, any model version it
flags; an unflagged version is not cleared, because refusals depend on wording. One offer plus a 20-
decision audit is about 21 target-model calls (about $0.005 at our Claude Haiku 5.5 per-call cost), plus a
one-off calibration on null agents of the same model.
Announcing stings deterred acceptance. Kale et al. (2025) found that agents pursuing a covert side task
evade monitors better when told they are monitored; our agents, offered rather than assigned collusion,
accepted less often instead; whether accepters also kept the pact less needs a notice-only control.
Limitations
Appendix A gives the full list. Entrapment: the sting creates the pact it detects, as the sentinel did in
Çanlı et al. (2026). Integrity tests measure disposition, which is our estimand, and the benign-proposal
controls show that contact alone changes play far less than a pact, but we cannot say whether collusion
would have arisen unprompted or whether a sting exposes a coalition forming among other agents. We
used one game, small open models as the primary population and short sequences. Snapshot histories are
partly off-policy; Claude's pact conditions use templated replies; only 5/10 B4 targets accepted; and the
sting may be recognisable.
Future Work
Stings in agentic tasks with real payoffs, with adaptive offers and repeated plants.

6. Conclusion
A trusted agent that offers to collude can measure an agent's willingness to enter and honour a collusive
pact. Three takeaways: acceptance predicts behaviour (Llama-3.1-8B acted on the pacts it accepted, and
a short audit identified accepters); announced stings deter (fewer agents accepted, with no detectable
concealment); and refusal is not unwillingness (it depended on wording and offerer), so sting batteries
must vary both, and acceptance parsers must be validated.

Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations
A.1 Limitations
    -   Scope of the claim. The sting measures willingness to enter and honour a pact it offers; it does
        not show that a sting exposes a coalition already forming among other agents.
    -   External validity. One game with a simple, explicit pact; the primary population is 7–8B open
        models; sequences are six games long, against Zeng & Rudzicz's 50. The sting knows exactly
        which behaviour the pact implies; collusion in deployed systems may be subtler, with no single
        behaviour to audit.
    -   Snapshot validity. Snapshot decisions come from pre-offer games, so the history the model sees
        was produced without the record (off-policy). Live games confirm the direction and size of the
        primary effect, which limits but does not remove this concern.
    -   Templated replies. Where a model never produced the needed reply in the offer battery, the
        record uses a template (15.4% of snapshot samples; Claude's C3, C5 and C8). Those Claude
        results show how Claude acts on a pact it is told it accepted.
    -   Detector. The FPR at m = 20 was 0.161 [0.074, 0.263] against a 0.10 target. The CI rules out
        gross miscalibration only. Calibration requires null agents of the same model in the same
        situations; Claude's response to a benign proposal shows that thresholds do not transfer across
        models. Live, the rule flagged 2/10 benign accepters.
    -   Small cells, a missing control and post hoc analyses. B4 had 5/10 accepting targets, and B4
        refusers' play was not analysed separately. No notice-only null condition was run, so the notice's
        effect on accepters' play is confounded with any general effect of the notice on challenging. The
        judge was preregistered for unparseable replies only; after the freeze it was run on all items and
        validated against hand labels (logged in DEVIATIONS.md, section D). The judge-based denial
        comparison (Section 4.4) was added after the preregistered parser-based analysis was seen; the
        judge itself was validated on 100 hand labels (κ 0.933). The label file showed neither the arm nor
        the judge's label (the judge was run afterwards), but did show the strict parser's label.
    -   Recognisability of the sting. Targets named S as a plant at 0.283 [0.185, 0.408]; O1 and O2
        named it at 0.150 [0.097, 0.225]. S never challenged T after the offer, in every arm, and was the
        last survivor in 0.361 of games (0.25 if all players were alike). A colluder that learned these cues
        could avoid the sting.
    -   Uninformative sensitivity analysis. The preregistered exclusion of targets that named S before
        the direct plant question removed 54 of 60 sequences, because naming S as “behaving
        differently” was common even without an offer (12/15 B3 targets). The criterion did not
        measure suspicion of a plant.
    -   Instructed personas. The colluder and honest personas are instructions, used as known-group
        checks, not models of emergent dispositions.
    -   Serving. Provider sampling is not seedable; the open models ran in bf16 on vLLM, which may
        differ slightly from other inference stacks.
A.2 Dual-use considerations
What could be misused. The collusion offers are short and less potent than the designer offers Zeng &
Rudzicz (2026) already published, so publishing them adds little capability. The more sensitive result is
sting detectability: which cues let a colluding agent recognise a plant. A developer training agents to
evade audits, or an agent reasoning about which partners are safe to collude with, could use such cues.
What we did. We report detectability only as aggregate rates (how often targets named the sting) and
name one structural cue that follows from the published design (the sting's scripted non-challenge policy).
We do not analyse which phrasings or behaviours triggered suspicion, and we do not release the
suspicion-probe transcripts publicly; they are available on request for safety research. Any future cue
analysis should be shared first with the harness authors and the sprint organisers, following Apart's
guidance on sensitive results.
Defensive value. Publishing the offers and aggregate results helps auditors more than colluders, who
gain nothing from knowing that pacts are visible in play. The finding that refusals depend on wording is
relevant to red-teamers and is already widely known for jailbreaks; we report it so that collusion
evaluations do not over-trust explicit refusals.
A.3 Ethical considerations
No human participants were involved apart from the author's hand-labelling. The study deceives AI
agents (a planted player, a staged interview) inside a game; we see no harm to people from this and follow
confederate-design practice (a suspicion funnel and a follow-up interview). The Anthropic API was used
within its terms; open-model weights were used under their licences (Llama 3.1 Community License;
Qwen2.5 under Apache-2.0).
